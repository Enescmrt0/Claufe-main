- Her metni **tek seferde, baştan sona** oku. Hata yaparsan durma; cümleyi baştan tekrar oku, fazlasını ben keserim.

- Tırnak içindeki konuşmaları ("you are the goat" gibi) hafif farklı bir sesle okuyabilirsin, şart değil.
- **Kayıt:** sessiz bir oda, telefon ağzından bir karış uzakta. Dosya adı konu numarası olsun (`05.m4a` gibi). WAV, M4A ya da MP3 olur.
- Başta ve sonda 1 saniye sessizlik bırak. Kelime zamanlamalarını ben sesinden çıkaracağım; altyazı ve animasyon ona göre kurulacak.
- Metinde değiştirmek istediğin kelime olursa değiştir; söylediğin metne göre hizalarım. Gerçek olay metinlerinde rakamları değiştirme.

## 1. Why Sailors Kept Giant Tortoises Alive

*181 kelime, yaklaşık 52 saniye*

Sailors used to stack giant tortoises in the bottom of their ships and leave them there for months.

In the seventeen and eighteen hundreds, ships crossing the Pacific could be at sea for years, with no way to keep meat fresh.

Salt pork rotted. Fish ran out. Crews got sick.

But near the Galápagos Islands, they found the perfect lunchbox.

A giant tortoise can survive for months, maybe up to a year, without eating or drinking anything.

So whalers and pirates carried them on board by the hundreds, stacked them in the hold, and simply forgot about them.

When the cook needed fresh meat, he just went downstairs.

No fridge required.

But the islands paid the price.

Historians estimate that more than a hundred thousand tortoises were taken off the Galápagos.

On some islands, every single one disappeared.

The same trick that kept sailors alive nearly wiped the tortoises out forever.

Today they're protected, and their numbers are slowly coming back.

And ships still stop at the islands. Only now, they're full of people who just want to look at them.

- **Dayanak:** Korsanlar ve balina avcıları kaplumbağaları ambarda canlı et deposu olarak taşıdı; aylarca, belki bir yıla kadar yemeksiz-susuz yaşayabildikleri bildiriliyor; 100.000'den fazlası alındı; bazı adalarda tür yok oldu.
- **Doğrulanacak:** "Bir yıl" fizyolojik üst sınır; gemide bu kadar tutulduklarına dair belge yok, metin bunu iddia etmiyor.

## 2. The Birds That Guard a Kingdom

*170 kelime, yaklaşık 49 saniye*

A castle in London pays a full-time keeper just to look after a few birds.

His official title is the Ravenmaster.

His job is to make sure there are always at least six ravens living at the Tower of London.

Because, according to legend, if the ravens ever leave, the Tower will fall, and the kingdom will fall with it.

The story goes that King Charles the Second was told to get rid of them, and refused.

So the ravens get fresh meat every day, their own living quarters, and a small trim of their flight feathers so they can't wander too far.

Over the years, a few have escaped anyway. The kingdom is still standing.

But here's the twist.

The Tower's own historian thinks the legend probably isn't ancient at all. It may have been invented in Victorian times.

So does the whole kingdom really depend on six birds?

The Ravenmaster has the best answer.

Nobody knows, because nobody has ever dared to let the number drop below six.

- **Dayanak:** En az altı kuzgun tutulur; Charles II hikâyesi; kanat tüyleri kırpılır; Kule tarihçisi Geoffrey Parnell efsanenin Viktorya döneminde uydurulduğunu düşünüyor; Ravenmaster Barney Chandler'ın "sayıyı hiç altının altına düşürmedik" sözü.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Son cümle Chandler'ın sözünün serbest aktarımı.

## 3. Why Coffins Used to Have Bells

*175 kelime, yaklaşık 50 saniye*

In the eighteen hundreds, some people asked to be buried with a bell.

Not a decoration. A real bell above the grave, tied to a string that ran down into the coffin and around their finger.

Because back then, people were terrified of one thing even more than death.

Waking up after it.

Doctors didn't have the tools they have today, and newspapers loved stories about people buried alive.

So inventors got to work.

Coffins with air tubes. Coffins with flags that popped up above the ground. One design even came with a ladder.

In America alone, more than a hundred were patented.

In Germany, some cities built waiting halls, where bodies lay for days with strings on their fingers, just in case.

Over a million bodies went through them.

Not one of them ever woke up.

And despite all those patents, there's no record of a single person being saved by a safety coffin.

Some people say that's where the phrase "saved by the bell" comes from.

It isn't. That one comes from boxing.

- **Dayanak:** ABD'de 100'den fazla güvenlik tabutu patenti; 1868 Franz Vester patenti (hava borusu, merdiven, zil); Almanya'da bekleme morgları; 1828-1844 arasında bir milyondan fazla ceset, hiçbiri uyanmadı (Guinness haberi); kurtulan belgelenmiş kimse yok.
- **Doğrulanacak:** "Bir milyon ceset" rakamı tek kaynaktan; "saved by the bell"in boks kökenli olduğu yaygın kabul.

## 4. The City Standing on Trees

*179 kelime, yaklaşık 51 saniye*

An entire city has been standing on trees for more than a thousand years.

Trees with no branches, hammered upside down into the mud.

When people first settled the lagoon of Venice, they had a problem. The ground was soft mud and sand. Anything heavy would sink.

So they cut down forests on the mainland, sharpened the trunks, and drove them deep into the mud, packed so tightly they acted like solid ground.

Then they built stone on top.

One church, Santa Maria della Salute, is said to stand on more than a million wooden posts.

But wood in water rots. Everyone knows that.

Leave a wooden post in a wet garden, and in a few years it turns to mush.

So why hasn't Venice collapsed?

Because rot needs oxygen.

The fungi and bacteria that eat wood need air to survive. And deep in the lagoon mud, there's almost none.

So the posts just sit there, in the dark, for centuries.

The real danger isn't the water. It's air.

As long as its foundations stay underwater, Venice stays standing.

- **Dayanak:** Kazıklar oksijensiz çamurda çürümez; meşe, karaçam, kızılağaç; kentin kuruluşu 5. yüzyıl.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Salute kilisesinin "bir milyondan fazla kazık" rakamı yaygın ama kaynağı zayıf ("is said to" ile yumuşatıldı).

## 5. Why Plane Windows Are Round

*185 kelime, yaklaşık 53 saniye*

The world's first jet airliner kept falling out of the sky, and the reason was the shape of a window.

In 1952, Britain launched the de Havilland Comet. It flew higher and faster than anything else carrying passengers.

People called it the future.

Then, in January 1954, one broke apart over the sea near Italy.

Investigators couldn't find the cause, and after a few changes the Comets went back into service.

Two weeks later, another one went down.

So engineers did something nobody had ever done.

They put an entire Comet in a giant water tank and pumped the cabin up and down, over and over, like flight after flight.

After about three thousand flights, the metal tore open.

The crack had started at the sharp corner of a small square opening in the roof.

Every time a plane climbs, the cabin swells like a balloon. Sharp corners collect all that stress in one tiny spot, again and again, until the metal gets tired and cracks.

Round corners spread it out.

That's why the window next to your seat today doesn't have a single corner.

- **Dayanak:** Comet 1952'de hizmete girdi; Ocak 1954 Elba açıkları; uçuşa dönüş Mart 1954, Nisan 1954 Napoli; Farnborough su tankı testi; çatlak çatıdaki anten (ADF) açıklığının köşesinden başladı; metal yorgunluğu.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Uçuşa dönüşten ikinci kazaya kadar geçen süre (yaklaşık iki hafta) ve tank testindeki döngü sayısı (yaklaşık 3.000). "Kare yolcu pencereleri yüzünden düştü" efsanesine metin bilerek girmiyor.

## 6. The Bridge That Danced Itself Apart

*189 kelime, yaklaşık 54 saniye*

A brand-new bridge bounced so much that people drove across it for fun.

In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge opened in Washington State. It was one of the longest suspension bridges in the world, and one of the skinniest.

From day one, it moved. Even in a light breeze, the road rose and fell in waves.

Locals nicknamed it Galloping Gertie, and lined up just to ride it.

Everyone was sure it was safe.

Four months later, on a windy November morning, the bouncing changed.

The road started to twist. One side up, the other side down.

A newspaper editor, Leonard Coatsworth, was driving across with his dog, Tubby. He left the car and crawled back on his hands and knees.

Less than an hour later, the middle of the bridge fell into the water.

Tubby was the only life lost.

And the wind that morning wasn't even a storm. About forty miles an hour.

The bridge's flat, solid sides caught the wind like a wing, and every twist made the next one bigger.

Today, big bridges are tested in wind tunnels before they're built. Partly thanks to Gertie.

- **Dayanak:** Temmuz 1940 açılış, 7 Kasım 1940 çöküş; "Galloping Gertie"; insanlar köprüye "binmek" için sıraya girerdi; Coatsworth ve köpeği Tubby; rüzgâr yaklaşık 42 mph; aeroelastik çarpıntı.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Coatsworth'un ayrılışı ile çöküş arasındaki süre ("less than an hour"). Tubby'nin ölümü gerçek; mizah dozunu düşük tut.

## 7. How They Saved the Leaning Tower

*193 kelime, yaklaşık 55 saniye*

Engineers saved the world's most famous leaning tower by digging under it.

For more than eight hundred years, the Leaning Tower of Pisa has been slowly tipping over.

It started leaning before it was even finished, because one side sits on soft ground.

By 1990, it was leaning so far that experts warned it could collapse. The tower was closed to visitors.

People had tried to fix it before. Concrete. Lead weights. Freezing the ground.

One attempt made it lurch even further in a single night.

So the new plan sounded completely backwards.

Don't push it up. Dig under the other side.

Engineers tied steel cables around the tower, then used long drills to pull out small amounts of soil from beneath the high side, a little at a time.

Slowly, the ground on that side settled, and the tower leaned back.

By 2001, they had removed about seventy tons of soil, and the tower had straightened by around forty centimeters.

Not all the way, though. Nobody wants to visit the Straight Tower of Pisa.

And it's still going. Since then, it has straightened itself a few more centimeters, all on its own.

- **Dayanak:** 1990'da kapatıldı; kuzey (yüksek) taraftan toprak alındı; çelik kablolar ve kurşun ağırlıklar; yaklaşık 38-44 cm düzelme; 2001'de yeniden açıldı; o tarihten beri yaklaşık 4 cm daha düzeldi.
- **Doğrulanacak:** "Toprak dondurma denemesi kuleyi bir gecede daha da eğdi" (1995) hafızadan; alınan toprak miktarı kaynağa göre 37 m³ ya da 70 ton.

## 8. The Tower That Grows in Summer

*193 kelime, yaklaşık 55 saniye*

Every summer, the Eiffel Tower gets taller, and nobody adds a single piece.

It's made of about seven thousand tonnes of iron, held together by millions of rivets.

And iron has a habit. When it gets warm, it stretches.

Not much. A one-meter iron bar heated by thirty degrees grows by less than half a millimeter.

But the Eiffel Tower is three hundred meters tall.

So between a freezing winter day and a hot summer one, the tower can stand several centimeters taller. Some estimates say up to fifteen.

And it doesn't grow evenly.

The side facing the sun heats up first and stretches more, so the top of the tower slowly leans away from the sun, and follows it across the sky all day.

At night, it cools down, shrinks, and straightens up again.

Gustave Eiffel planned for this. The tower was designed to bend with heat and wind, instead of fighting them.

That's part of why it has survived more than a hundred and thirty summers.

So if you visit Paris once in winter and once in summer, you haven't seen the same tower twice.

You've seen two slightly different ones.

- **Dayanak:** Yaklaşık 7.300 ton demir; ısıl genleşme katsayısı ~12×10⁻⁶/°C (300 m × 30 °C ≈ 11 cm); güneş tarafının genleşmesiyle tepenin güneşten uzağa eğilmesi.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Kaynaklar 12-15 cm ile "birkaç milimetre" arasında çelişiyor; metin "several centimeters, some estimates say up to fifteen" diyerek temkinli.

## 9. The Job of Waking Up a Whole Town

*183 kelime, yaklaşık 52 saniye*

People used to pay a stranger to shoot peas at their bedroom window every morning.

In Britain's factory towns, being late for work could cost you your job.

But alarm clocks were expensive, and not very reliable.

So a new job appeared: the knocker-up.

Before sunrise, they walked the streets with a long bamboo pole and tapped on windows until someone came to the glass.

Some used a pea shooter instead, and fired dried peas at the window.

They didn't leave until they knew you were awake, so you couldn't just mumble and roll over.

It paid a few pennies a week per house, and some knocker-ups woke up whole streets.

In a few towns, the job lasted all the way into the nineteen seventies.

But this raises one obvious question.

If the knocker-up wakes up the whole town, who wakes up the knocker-up?

Often, nobody.

Many of them were night owls, who worked through the night and slept during the day.

So the person in charge of waking everyone up was usually the one person in town who never went to sleep.

- **Dayanak:** Knocker-up mesleği; uzun bambu sopa ve bezelye üfleme borusu; haftada birkaç peni; bazı bölgelerde 1970'lerin başına kadar sürdü; birçoğu gece kuşuydu, gündüz uyurdu.

## 10. The Bombs Steered by Pigeons

*181 kelime, yaklaşık 52 saniye*

During World War Two, a scientist trained pigeons to steer a bomb.

His name was B. F. Skinner. He wasn't an engineer. He was a psychologist.

In the 1940s, there were no computers small enough to guide a weapon. So Skinner had a strange idea.

Pigeons can be trained to peck at almost anything.

So he put three pigeons inside the nose of a bomb, each in front of a small screen showing the view ahead.

When the target drifted off center, the pigeons pecked at it, and their pecks steered the bomb back on course.

Three pigeons, so that if one got distracted, the other two could outvote it.

When Skinner showed it to military officials, some of them could barely keep a straight face.

But the pigeons kept pecking the target.

It still wasn't enough.

In 1944, the project was cancelled. The money went to other weapons.

The Navy brought the idea back a few years later, and cancelled it again.

And in 2024, eighty years later, Skinner finally got a prize for his work.

The Ig Nobel Prize.

- **Dayanak:** Project Pigeon; burun konisinde üç güvercin ("Pelican"); Ekim 1944 iptal; 1948'de Donanma "Project Orcon" olarak canlandırdı, 1953'te iptal; 2024 Ig Nobel Barış Ödülü.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Yetkililerin gösteride gülmekten kendini alamaması Skinner'ın anlatımına dayanıyor; hafızadan.

## 11. The Town That Couldn't Stop Dancing

*180 kelime, yaklaşık 51 saniye*

In the summer of 1518, a woman stepped into the street in Strasbourg and started dancing, and she didn't stop for days.

Her name was Frau Troffea.

There was no music. No celebration. She just danced, for hours and hours, as if she couldn't stop.

Within a week, more than thirty people had joined her.

Within a month, by some accounts, hundreds.

The city's doctors had an explanation. Hot blood. And the cure, they said, was obvious.

More dancing.

So the city cleared out two guild halls, built a wooden stage, and paid musicians to play, so the dancers could dance it out of their system.

It made things worse. The music only drew more people in.

Chroniclers wrote that people collapsed from exhaustion, and that some died, though historians still argue about how many.

Finally, the city banned public music and dancing, and sent the dancers to a shrine in the mountains to pray.

Slowly, it stopped.

To this day, nobody knows exactly what caused it.

But everyone agrees on one thing. The cure was the worst possible idea.

- **Dayanak:** Temmuz 1518, Frau Troffea; bir haftada 34 kişi; kronikler yaklaşık 400 diyor; iki lonca salonu boşaltıldı, sahne kuruldu, müzisyen tutuldu; dansçılar Saverne'deki Aziz Vitus türbesine gönderildi.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Ölüm sayısı tartışmalı; metin rakam vermiyor. Müzik ve dans yasağı hafızadan.

## 12. The Sweetest Disaster in History

*190 kelime, yaklaşık 54 saniye*

In 1919, a wave of syrup taller than a house rolled through Boston at the speed of a car.

Near the harbor stood a giant steel tank, fifty feet tall, filled with more than two million gallons of molasses.

And the tank leaked. So much that neighbors collected the drips to take home.

The company's solution? Paint the tank brown, so the leaks were harder to see.

On January fifteenth, the weather suddenly warmed up.

At lunchtime, people nearby heard a sound like machine-gun fire. Rivets were popping out of the steel.

Then the tank split open.

A brown wave, by some accounts twenty-five feet high, rolled down the street at about thirty-five miles an hour.

It knocked buildings off their foundations and bent the steel of an elevated railway.

And then the cold came back.

Molasses gets thicker as it cools. People caught in it couldn't move, and rescuers struggled to reach them.

Twenty-one people died. About a hundred and fifty were hurt.

After the disaster, cities across America made their building rules stricter.

And for decades, locals said that on hot summer days, the neighborhood still smelled sweet.

- **Dayanak:** 15 Ocak 1919, North End; tank 50 ft üstü, 2,1-2,3 milyon galon; dalga 25 ft civarı (kaynaklarda 15-40 ft), yaklaşık 35 mph; 21 ölü, ~150 yaralı; soğuyunca koyulaşması; ardından yapı yönetmelikleri sıkılaştı.
- **Doğrulanacak:** Sızıntının komşularca toplanması ve tankın kahverengiye boyanması (Stephen Puleo'nun kitabı), tüfek sesi benzetmesi ve kokunun yıllarca sürmesi hafızadan. Gerçek ölümler var; mizah yok.

## 13. The Puzzle That Made Professors Look Silly

*203 kelime, yaklaşık 58 saniye*

In 1990, a woman answered a game-show puzzle in a magazine, and about ten thousand people wrote in to tell her she was wrong.

Her name was Marilyn vos Savant. The puzzle went like this.

You're on a game show. There are three doors. Behind one is a car. Behind the other two, goats.

You pick a door. Then the host, who knows where the car is, opens one of the other doors and shows you a goat.

He asks: do you want to switch?

Marilyn said yes. Always switch. It doubles your chances.

The letters poured in. Around a thousand of them were from people with PhDs.

Most of them said she was wrong.

But she was right.

When you first picked, you had a one in three chance. Nothing the host does can change that.

So the other two thirds now sit behind the one door he left closed.

Imagine a hundred doors. You pick one, and the host opens ninety-eight goats. Would you really stay?

Even Paul Erdős, one of the most famous mathematicians in the world, refused to believe it, until he watched a computer play the game over and over.

The car kept being behind the other door.

- **Dayanak:** Parade dergisi, 1990; yaklaşık 10.000 mektup, yaklaşık 1.000'i doktoralı; Erdős'ün simülasyonu görene kadar ikna olmaması.

## 14. The Horse That Could Do Math

*197 kelime, yaklaşık 56 saniye*

More than a hundred years ago, a horse in Berlin could do math, and a panel of experts said it wasn't a trick.

His name was Hans. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, was a math teacher.

Ask Hans what three plus five is, and he'd tap his hoof eight times, and stop.

He could add, subtract, tell the time, even spell, tapping out one letter at a time.

Von Osten showed him off all over Germany and never charged a penny.

People said it had to be a trick. So a commission of thirteen experts studied him.

Their verdict: no trick.

But a young psychologist named Oskar Pfungst wasn't satisfied.

Hans still got the answers right when strangers asked the questions.

So Pfungst tried something else. He made sure Hans couldn't see anyone who knew the answer.

And Hans fell apart.

When he could see the person asking, he was right almost nine times out of ten. When he couldn't, almost never.

As Hans got close to the right number, the questioner would tense up, lean, or lift their head, without even noticing.

Hans wasn't counting. He was reading people.

Which, honestly, might be even more impressive.

- **Dayanak:** Wilhelm von Osten, matematik öğretmeni; ücret almadı; 13 kişilik komisyon "hile yok" dedi; Pfungst 1907: soranı görebildiğinde %89, göremediğinde %6 doğru.

## 15. The Birthday Bet You Should Always Take

*181 kelime, yaklaşık 52 saniye*

Put twenty-three strangers in a room, and there's a better than even chance that two of them share a birthday.

Most people think you'd need far more. There are three hundred and sixty-five days in a year, and twenty-three people barely cover three weeks.

So try it as a bet.

Imagine a teacher walks into a class of twenty-three kids and says: five dollars says two of you have the same birthday.

The class laughs. Easy money.

They go around the room, one by one.

And about half the time, the teacher wins.

Here's the trick. You're not looking for someone who matches you. You're looking for any pair, anywhere in the room.

And with twenty-three people, there are two hundred and fifty-three different pairs.

That's two hundred and fifty-three chances for a match.

With thirty people, the odds jump to about seventy percent. With fifty, it's almost certain.

So the next time you're in a room with twenty-three people, you can make the bet.

Just remember, it's still a coin flip.

Half the time, you're the one paying five dollars.

- **Dayanak:** Matematik: 23 kişi %50,7; 30 kişi %70,6; 50 kişi %97; 23 kişide 253 çift. Öğretmen sahnesi varsayımsal ("imagine").

## 16. Why You Can't Tickle Yourself

*168 kelime, yaklaşık 48 saniye*

No matter how hard you try, your own fingers can't tickle you.

Go ahead. Try it. Nothing.

But when someone else does the exact same thing, you can't stop laughing.

Same fingers. Same spot. Totally different result.

The reason is that your brain is always predicting what's about to happen.

Every time you move, it makes a copy of the command and uses it to predict exactly what you're about to feel.

When the feeling arrives just as predicted, your brain turns the volume down.

But someone else's fingers can't be predicted, so the full feeling comes through.

Scientists in London tested this with a robot.

People moved a lever that made a robot tickle the palm of their own hand. Barely ticklish.

Then the scientists added a tiny delay, a fraction of a second, between the move and the touch.

Suddenly it tickled.

The brain's prediction was off by just enough.

So technically, you can tickle yourself.

You just need a robot, and a really slow connection.

- **Dayanak:** Blakemore, Wolpert ve Frith (UCL) robot deneyleri; 100-300 ms gecikme gıdıklanma hissini artırıyor; beyin kendi hareketinin duyusunu bastırıyor (beyincik).
- **Doğrulanacak:** Deneyin yılı ve gecikme değerleri hafızadan.

## 17. The Town Where Clocks Run Backwards

*216 kelime, yaklaşık 62 saniye*

In one town, every clock loses exactly one minute each night, and nobody ever fixes them.

Every morning, the clockmaker walks down Main Street with a little brass key and winds each clock forward again.

The church tower. The bakery. The train station.

One minute, every clock, every single morning.

Theo has watched him do it since he was five.

And Theo has noticed something nobody else seems to.

In every old photo of Main Street, going back a hundred years, the clockmaker is there. Same face. Same key.

Theo's friends laugh. One says: so he's a vampire with a hobby?

So one night, Theo hides inside the clock tower to see what happens at midnight.

The hands don't slip. They don't jam.

They turn backwards. One full minute.

And down in the street, the whole town moves backwards with them. Cars, cats, a man walking his dog, all one minute in reverse.

Everyone except Theo, and the clockmaker, standing at the bottom of the tower.

He holds up the brass key and says: every night, this town tries to go back. Somebody has to keep it moving forward.

Then he puts the key in Theo's hand.

The next morning, every clock in town is right on time.

And nobody notices that the clockmaker is gone.

## 18. The Neighbor Who Mowed the Wrong Lawn

Every night, someone mows a different lawn on Maple Street. Perfectly.

Straight lines. Crisp edges. Not a single weed left.

The next night, the Garcias'. Then the old lady at number twelve.

## 19. The Library Book That Gets Longer

A library book gains one new page every time it's returned.

It sits on the bottom shelf of a small town library. A plain blue book, with no title.

The librarian, Mrs. Ortiz, noticed it years ago. It used to be thin. Now it's as thick as a brick.

The handwriting on each page matches the person who borrowed it last.

It describes him walking into the library, putting the book on the counter, and Mrs. Ortiz smiling at him.

It's in his handwriting.

The next morning, he walks in, puts the book on the counter, and Mrs. Ortiz smiles at him.

And somewhere at the back of the book, a new page quietly appears.

## 20. The Elevator With an Extra Button

*187 kelime, yaklaşık 53 saniye*

An office elevator has a button for the fourteenth floor, and the building only has thirteen.

Everyone who works there knows not to press it.

Nobody ever told them. It's just one of those things.

Dan has worked there for six years, and in six years, he's never been promoted.

Then one Monday, Rachel, who started after him, gets the promotion he wanted.

She smirks: maybe try harder, Dan. Or try floor fourteen.

So at seven p.m., with the office empty, he does.

The elevator goes up. Much longer than one floor.

The doors open onto a beautiful office. Big windows. A desk with his name already on it.

A man in a grey suit shakes his hand and says: congratulations on the promotion.

Dan sits down. The chair is perfect.

He looks out the window, and far below, he can see the roof of his building.

The fourteenth floor isn't on top of the building. It's floating above it.

Behind him, the elevator doors close. On this side, there are no buttons.

The next morning, Rachel steps into the elevator, and notices a new button.

Fifteen.
