# Apollo 13: Four Days to Get Home

Gerçek olay. 62 cümle, 871 kelime, yaklaşık 5.4 dakika. Yatay 16:9, `/stick-figure` motoru.

## 1. The bang (APRIL 13, 1970)

Two hundred thousand miles from Earth, three men hear a loud bang.

The spacecraft shudders. Warning lights flash across the panel.

Then one of them picks up the radio and says the most famous understatement in history.

Houston, we've had a problem.

What follows are four days in which almost everything goes wrong, and a few hundred people on the ground refuse to give up.

## 2. The routine mission (APRIL 11)

Apollo 13 was supposed to be the third mission to land on the Moon.

By 1970, people were already getting bored of Moon landings. The TV networks barely covered the launch.

The crew was Jim Lovell, the commander, Fred Haise, and Jack Swigert.

Swigert wasn't even supposed to be there.

Three days before launch, the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to measles, and was swapped out.

He never got sick.

## 3. What blew up (55 HOURS IN)

Two days into the flight, mission control asks the crew to stir the oxygen tanks, a routine chore.

Swigert flips the switch.

Sixteen seconds later, oxygen tank number two explodes.

Years earlier, that tank had been dropped about two inches during a test. And its heater switches were built for twenty-eight volts, but on the ground they were run at sixty-five.

They welded shut, and cooked the wiring inside the tank.

For months, it sat there like a bomb with no timer. Until someone stirred it.

## 4. Losing the ship (10:10 PM)

The explosion blew a whole panel off the side of the service module.

Out of the window, Lovell sees something bad. A cloud of gas, streaming into space.

It's their oxygen. The same oxygen that makes their electricity and their water.

One by one, the gauges fall. The command module, their only way home, has a few hours of power left.

The Moon landing is over. Now there is only one question. Can they get home alive?

## 5. The lifeboat (11:00 PM)

There is one place left to go. The lunar module, nicknamed Aquarius.

It was built to carry two men down to the Moon and back up, for about two days.

Now it has to keep three men alive for four.

The crew powers down the command module completely, to save it for the very end, and moves into a vehicle the size of two phone booths.

It's like using your lifeboat as the whole ship.

## 6. Going around (DAY 3)

They can't just turn around. Space doesn't work like that.

Instead, they have to swing around the back of the Moon, and let its gravity throw them home, like a slingshot.

Behind the Moon, they lose radio contact with Earth.

For a while, they are farther from home than any human beings have ever been.

Then they fire the lunar module's engine to speed up the trip, and come out the other side, headed for Earth.

## 7. The square peg (DAY 3)

But a new problem is quietly building up. The air.

Every breath they take adds carbon dioxide. Too much, and they will pass out, and never wake up.

The lunar module's filters were made for two people, and they are filling up fast.

The command module has plenty of spare filters. There's just one problem.

Its filters are square. The lunar module's holes are round.

So on the ground, engineers dump everything the crew has on board onto a table. Plastic bags. Cardboard. Hoses. A sock. Duct tape.

They have to build a square filter that fits a round hole, using only that pile.

They figure it out, and read the instructions up to the crew, step by step.

The crew builds it. The carbon dioxide starts to fall.

## 8. Cold (DAY 4)

Now, to save power, almost everything is switched off. No heaters.

The cabin gets close to freezing. Water condenses on the walls.

The crew can barely sleep. They drink very little, to save water.

Haise comes down with a fever. They are cold, exhausted, and running out of time.

And the trajectory keeps drifting. To fix it, they have to fire the engine by hand, steering by the edge of the Earth in the window.

It works.

## 9. Waking the ship (APRIL 17)

Now comes the hardest part. The command module has been frozen and dead for days.

Nobody has ever restarted one in flight. There was no checklist for it.

So on the ground, Ken Mattingly, the pilot who was left behind because of measles, works in a simulator for hours, figuring out how to wake the ship up without blowing the tiny amount of power left.

His procedure works.

The crew climbs back into the command module, and says goodbye to the lifeboat that saved them.

## 10. Splashdown (1:07 PM)

The capsule hits the atmosphere, wrapped in fire. The radio goes silent, as it always does.

Normally, the silence lasts about four minutes.

Four minutes pass. Nothing.

Mission control is completely still.

Then, a voice. The parachutes are open.

Apollo 13 splashes down in the Pacific Ocean, and Jim Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert are home.

## 11. The successful failure (AFTER)

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon. NASA calls it a successful failure.

The famous line, failure is not an option, was actually written for the movie, years later.

What really happened was quieter. People who refused to panic, and solved one problem at a time, with whatever they had.

A tank of oxygen nearly killed them. A sock, some duct tape and a lot of people who wouldn't quit brought them home.
